These are the principles I try to invest by. Most of them come from the [Bogleheads getting started guide](https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Getting_started), and none of them are complicated. The hard part is following them for decades.

This is a record of how I think, not financial advice.

## Make a plan and keep to it

Research on US mutual funds shows that the investors in a fund tend to do far worse than the fund itself. The fund doesn't change. The investors do: they buy after prices have risen and sell after prices have fallen.

So the first rule is discipline. Write down a sensible plan, then follow it. A good plan followed consistently has produced good results over the long term, and a brilliant plan abandoned in a bad year has not.

## Stay the course

Stocks have paid high returns over time, and the price of those returns is volatility. Big swings are part of the deal, not a sign that something has broken.

In practice this means:

- Expect the market to be enormously volatile, and decide in advance that you will hold through it.
- Tune out the noise. Headlines are written to make you act, and acting is usually the mistake.
- Put your attention on rebalancing. It has to be done every year, and it is the one regular action the plan actually asks for.

## Why this is harder than it sounds

Neuroeconomics studies how the mind handles money, and its findings are not flattering. Mental habits and emotional reactions that serve us well in the rest of life work against us in markets. Fear, overconfidence, and the urge to follow the crowd all push toward buying high and selling low.

Knowing this doesn't make the feelings go away. It does make the case for a written plan, since a plan decides for you on the days when your judgment is at its worst.

## Keep costs low

Costs are the one part of investing I control completely. An expense ratio of 0.15% and one of 1.5% look close together, but the gap compounds every year for an entire investing lifetime.

As an illustration, take $10,000 left alone for 30 years at a 7% annual return before fees. At a 0.15% expense ratio it grows to about $73,000. At 1.5% it grows to about $50,000. The fee difference took nearly a third of the ending balance.

This is why low-cost index funds, such as an S&P 500 fund like VOO or SPY, sit at the center of my portfolio.

## Keep taxes low

Taxes are the other drag I can do something about.

- **Put each holding in the right account.** Some funds are more tax-efficient than others, so where a holding lives matters. The Bogleheads wiki has a guide to [tax-efficient fund placement](https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Tax-efficient_fund_placement).
- **Harvest losses.** Tax-loss harvesting turns a market downturn into an immediate tax saving, which is some consolation in a bad year.
- **Know how bonds are taxed.** Interest on US Treasury bonds and bills is free from state and local taxes. Municipal bond interest is generally free from federal tax, and often from state tax too when the bonds come from your own state.

## The short version

Make a plan. Own low-cost index funds. Keep taxes down. Rebalance once a year. Then leave it alone, especially when leaving it alone is uncomfortable.
